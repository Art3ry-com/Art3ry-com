AI Phone Intake: What the Call Should Capture Before You Call Back

Answering the call is half the job. The other half is what you know when it ends. Here is what a good intake captures, the questions worth asking, and how the record should reach you.

Answering is not intake

Most conversations about phone automation stop at pick up the call. That is the easy part. A call that gets answered and ends with a name and a vague I need a plumber is still a chore waiting for you at 6 PM: call back, ask the same questions, play phone tag, hope they have not called someone else.

Intake is what happens between hello and goodbye. It is the structured set of questions that turns a stranger's problem into a record you can act on without another conversation. If you want the plain-English version of the whole category, start with what an AI receptionist is. This post is about the part that decides whether the call was worth anything.

I run a one-person California field-services company, and over 700 jobs have gone through a system I built to run exactly this. What follows is what I learned about which questions earn their place.

The principle: ask only what changes your next move

Every question you ask on a call costs you a little of the caller's patience. A caller who just wants the leak fixed will tolerate three or four questions and abandon at ten. So the rule is simple: ask only what changes what you do next.

If the answer does not change the price, the schedule, the tools you bring, or whether you take the job at all, do not ask it on the first call. Save it for the visit.

The six things worth capturing

One, who and how to reach them. Name, the number they are calling from, and the best way and time to reach them. Confirm the number by reading it back, because a single wrong digit kills the lead.

Two, where. The service address or at least the city and cross street. Location decides whether you can take the job, how long the drive is, and whether it falls inside your service area. For California trades, this is also where the Central Valley sprawl bites: a call from Reedley and a call from Clovis are different afternoons.

Three, what. The problem in the caller's own words, plus one clarifying question that matters in your trade. A plumber wants to know if water is actively running. A salon wants the service and the stylist. A notary wants the document type and number of signers.

Four, when. How soon they need it and the windows that work. This is the field that separates the emergency you drop everything for from the quote you can handle tomorrow.

Five, how they found you. One quick question. It costs three seconds and tells you which of your efforts is working, which is data you cannot get any other way.

Six, what happens next. The caller should hang up knowing exactly what comes after: a text with a link, a callback by a stated time, a confirmed slot. Uncertainty after the call is what sends people back to Google.

Where the record should land

A transcript in an inbox is better than voicemail, but it is still homework. The record should arrive where you already work, already sorted: a text or notification with the three lines that matter, the full details in your calendar or job list, and the lead tagged by urgency so you can tell an emergency from a tire-kicker at a glance.

This is the point where most DIY setups quietly fail, because the voice part is easy and the plumbing is not. I wrote up where the connections tend to break in AI phone agent integrations, and what it really costs to wire it yourself in the DIY voice AI cost breakdown.

What to automate after the call ends

The minute the call ends, send the caller a text that confirms what you heard and states the next step. It catches mistakes while they are cheap to fix and tells the caller you are real and attentive. If you need photos or a document, ask for them in that text rather than on the phone.

If a callback is promised, the system should hold you to it. A task with a deadline, a nudge if it slips, and an automatic message to the customer if you are running behind. This is the follow-up side of the system, and it is what lead follow-up is built to do.

Do not automate judgment. Pricing exceptions, scope arguments, and angry customers belong to you. A good intake sends those up the line immediately, with context, instead of trying to handle them.

What good intake sounds like

It sounds like a competent person who values the caller's time. It greets, states the business, asks what is going on, and responds to the answer rather than reading a script. It confirms the details back. It never pretends to be a person if asked, and it hands off to a human when the caller wants one.

Test it the way a customer would. Call at 9 PM on a Sunday and be difficult. Mumble an address. Change your mind about the date. Ask something off-script. If the record you receive is still usable, the intake works. If you have to call back to figure out what happened, it does not.

Where to start

Write down the six fields above for your trade, in order of importance. Cut anything that does not change your next move. That list is your intake, whether a person, a form, or a voice system is collecting it.

FAQ

What is AI phone intake?
It is an automated voice agent that answers a call, asks a short set of structured questions, and delivers a usable job record to you. The goal is a call that ends with a name, location, problem, timing, and a confirmed next step.
What questions should an intake call ask?
Only the ones that change your next move: who and how to reach them, where, what the problem is, when they need it, how they found you, and what happens next. Anything else can wait for the visit.
How is intake different from an answering service?
An answering service usually takes a message. Intake collects structured information, texts a confirmation, and puts the record where your calendar and follow-up can act on it.
Can AI intake handle emergencies?
It can recognize urgency from the caller's answers and alert you immediately with the details. Decisions about price, scope, and anything sensitive should still go to a person.
Will callers know they are talking to AI?
A good setup is honest about it if asked and hands off to a human on request. What callers actually care about is being answered quickly and having their problem understood.
