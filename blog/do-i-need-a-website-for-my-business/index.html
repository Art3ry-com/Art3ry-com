Do I Need a Website for My Business? An Honest Answer

A claimed Google profile and a strong referral flow will carry some businesses a long way. Here is exactly where that runs out, and what the smallest useful website really looks like.

The short answer, including when it is no

You do not need a website to exist on Google, to take payments, or to run a profitable business. Plenty of trades run for years on a claimed Google Business Profile, good reviews, and referrals. If that is you and you are booked, I will tell you to keep your money rather than sell you a site.

You need a website at the moment strangers have to decide about you without talking to you first. That is the whole test. Referrals arrive pre-trusted, so they need very little from you. A stranger who found you in a search has three or four competitors open in other tabs and is deciding in about fifteen seconds which of you looks like a real operating business.

So the honest version is: a Google Business Profile plus a strong referral flow beats a bad website, every time. A good website beats both, because it is the only asset in that list you actually own.

The rest of this page is where the line sits. What a profile does and where it stops, when the answer is genuinely 'not yet', when it is an obvious yes, and what the smallest website that actually works looks like.

No, you do not need a website to be on Google

This is the most common misunderstanding I run into, so let me be blunt about it. A Google Business Profile is free, it does not require a website, and it is what puts you in the map results. You claim it, you verify it, and you are on Google. The website field on the profile is optional and you can leave it empty.

Verification is the part that trips people up, not the website. Depending on your category and history, Google verifies by postcard, phone, email, or video, and video verification is increasingly common for service businesses. Until verification completes, you will not appear, and that has nothing to do with whether you have a site.

You can also point that website field at something other than a website: a booking page, a Facebook page, a scheduling link. It works. What you lose is a real destination where you control what a stranger reads about you, and one of the clearer relevance signals you can send about what you actually do and where.

One thing that is no longer an option: Google used to generate a free single-page website from your Business Profile, and it retired that. Those addresses now point back at the profile itself. If the plan was to lean on the free Google-built site, that plan is gone. The mechanics of getting found locally are in SEO for local business.

What a Google Business Profile does, and where it runs out

A profile is genuinely powerful. It wins the map pack, it holds your reviews, it shows hours and photos, it takes calls and direction requests and messages, and it costs nothing. For a lot of local service businesses it is the single highest-return thing you own, and if yours is not claimed and filled out, do that before you read another word about websites.

Where it runs out is specificity. A profile cannot make your case at length. It cannot explain a process that justifies charging more than the cheapest option. It cannot host the answer to the awkward question that loses you jobs. It cannot show a stranger the six photos of similar work that would have closed them.

It also cannot rank for questions. Somebody typing 'plumber near me' will find your profile. Somebody typing what a specific job usually costs, or whether a permit is needed, or how long a thing takes, will find whoever wrote the answer. Those searches are earlier in the decision and they are where you become the obvious choice before anyone compares prices.

And you do not own it. A suspension, a category change, a competitor filing edits, and your presence changes without your consent. If the profile is your only address, a bad week at Google is a bad quarter in your business. Keeping it current is worth automating, which I covered in local SEO automation.

When the honest answer is 'not yet'

You are brand new and have no completed work. A website made entirely of adjectives converts worse than no website at all, because a visitor can feel the absence of proof. Do five jobs, take real photos, ask all five for a review, and then build. You will build a better site in a month than you would today, and it will cost you the same.

You are booked to capacity. A website's job is to create demand. If you are turning work away, more demand is not your constraint and a site will not help. Raise your prices, tighten your scheduling, or fix the part of the job that eats your evenings. Come back to the website when you have room.

You cannot finish this sentence: 'I do X for Y, and it usually takes Z.' If the offer is not clear in your own head, a website just publishes the confusion at scale. Every unclear site I have ever been asked to fix was an unclear offer first, and no amount of design fixed it.

Cash is genuinely tight. In that case, the order is: claim and complete the Google profile, get email on your own domain, put ten real photos of your work somewhere public, and build a habit of asking every finished customer for a review. That stack costs almost nothing and outperforms a $4,000 site for a business at that stage. It also builds the raw material the site will need later.

When it is a yes, and it is not close

You are paying for traffic. If you spend on ads, or buy leads from a platform, every dollar sends someone to a page. If that page is a profile you do not control or a competitor's marketplace listing, you are renting an audience and handing back the relationship. Paid traffic without a destination you own is the most expensive mistake in this category.

Your work needs explaining. Higher-ticket, technical, or unfamiliar services get researched before anybody calls. If your customer has to understand something before they are comfortable buying, that understanding has to live somewhere, and a profile has nowhere to put it.

Your competitors show up and you do not. Search a few of the things your customers actually type. If three competitors have pages answering those questions and you appear only when someone types your business name, you are invisible for every search that is not already about you.

You want inbound to enter a system instead of a voicemail. A website form that lands in a follow-up sequence beats a missed call every time, especially outside business hours. Pair it with something that answers when you cannot, and the after-hours enquiry stops evaporating. That is the argument in what an AI receptionist actually is.

And you want to stop being a tenant. Profiles, marketplaces, and social pages are all ground you rent. A domain you own with your content on it is the only piece of this that cannot be taken away by someone else's policy change.

Trust is the conversion mechanism, not design

A stranger landing on your site is running four checks in a few seconds, in this order. Is this a real operating business? Do they do exactly my thing? Have they done it for somebody like me? What happens if I reach out? Everything that answers one of those questions earns its place, and everything else is decoration.

The things that answer them are unglamorous. Real photos of your actual work, not stock. A full name and a face. License, registration, or certification numbers if your trade has them. The service area stated plainly. Reviews quoted with names attached. A straight answer about price, even if that answer is a range or a reason you quote after seeing the job. A described process, step by step, so the reader knows what the first week looks like.

The things that do not answer them are the things most sites are made of. Stock photography of a smiling team that is not yours. Phrases like 'passionate about excellence'. A hero carousel. An awards badge nobody recognizes. A mission statement. None of this moves anyone, because none of it is checkable.

The single highest-converting element on most small business sites is a clear answer to 'what happens next'. Tell them you reply within a few hours, tell them what you will ask, tell them what it costs to find out. Uncertainty is what stops people, and specificity is what removes it.

Your site versus Facebook, Instagram, and the lead marketplaces

A Facebook page can rank, can hold reviews, and can convert. It also puts your storefront behind someone else's login prompt, buries your contact details in a layout you do not control, changes that layout without asking, and can be disabled with no appeal and no warning. If your entire business address is a Facebook page, you have a single point of failure that you cannot inspect.

The lead marketplaces are worse in a specific way. You pay for the lead, they sell the same lead to three of your competitors, and the customer's relationship stays with the platform. You are buying a race, not a customer. That model works for filling gaps in a schedule and does not work as a foundation.

The practical rule is simple: use platforms for reach, own the address people land on. Post on social, be in the map results, appear in the marketplaces if the math works. Send all of it to one place that belongs to you.

The cheapest ownership move has nothing to do with a website. Get email on your own domain. A quote arriving from a free webmail address and the same quote arriving from your business domain get read very differently, and the second one costs a few dollars a month.

A bad website is worse than no website

A visitor who finds nothing forms no opinion of you. A visitor who finds a neglected site concludes you are barely operating, and that conclusion is much harder to reverse. This is the one case where doing nothing genuinely beats doing something badly.

The failure list is consistent. A layout that breaks on a phone. A page that takes six seconds to load on cellular data. A copyright year three years old in the footer. A contact form that has never been tested and quietly goes nowhere. A phone number you changed. Pages that still say 'coming soon'. A template with another city's name still in the text. A chat widget nobody answers.

Run the test yourself, right now, honestly. Open your own site on your phone, off wifi, the way a customer standing in a parking lot would. Time how long it takes to load. Find your own contact details. Send yourself an enquiry and see whether it arrives and how long you take to answer it.

Most owners discover something broken in that five-minute test. Fixing what you find is cheaper than a rebuild and usually recovers more work than a redesign would, which is a large part of why I look at the existing site before quoting anything.

Cleaning, coaching, and bookkeeping specifically

Cleaning. Mostly local and map-driven, so a claimed profile with steady reviews carries you a long way with residential customers. The site earns its keep the moment you want commercial contracts, because a property manager or a facilities coordinator will not award a recurring contract off a Facebook page. They want to see insurance, a service checklist, and evidence you handle buildings. A simple quote form also ends the phone tag that kills residential bookings.

Coaching. Here the website is close to the whole business. There is no map pack to win and often no local intent at all, so nothing is putting you in front of anyone by geography. The entire sale is trust in a specific person, and that requires long-form: who you help, what changes for them, what it costs, what the first session looks like, and real testimonials from named people. One strong page with genuine proof beats a ten-page site with none.

Bookkeeping. Buyers are checking credibility and safety before anything else, because they are about to hand you access to their money. Credentials, the software you work in, a clearly scoped engagement, a real business email, and a plain statement of how you handle their data. This trade is heavily referral-driven, so the site's job is often to confirm a referral rather than generate one, and confirming a referral is worth a great deal since that is the visitor most likely to buy.

The pattern across all three: the more your customer has to trust you before they pay, the more the website matters. The more your customer decides by proximity and price, the more the profile matters.

The smallest website that actually works

One page is enough to start. At the top: what you do, who you do it for, where you do it, and one obvious action. Not a slogan. A sentence a stranger could repeat to somebody else correctly.

Then proof. Three to five photos of your real work, three reviews with names, and any license or certification number your trade uses. Then the services, written in the words customers use rather than the words your industry uses. Then price, handled honestly: a range, a starting-from, or a plain explanation of why you quote after seeing the job. Silence on price reads as expensive.

Then contact. One method that reaches you fast and one that captures after hours into something that follows up, so the eleven at night enquiry is still there in the morning. Then a footer with your legal business name, service area, and an email address on your own domain.

That is a weekend on a builder or a small paid build, and it will out-convert most of the expensive sites it sits next to. FAQ

Do I have to have a website for my business?
No. There is no legal or platform requirement to have one, and businesses run profitably without one every day on referrals and a claimed Google profile. It becomes necessary at the point where people who have never met you are deciding whether to contact you, because that decision needs proof and a profile can only hold so much of it.
Do I need a website for Google My Business?
No. A Google Business Profile is free, does not require a website, and the website field on it is optional. You can leave it blank or point it at a booking link or a social page. Leaving it blank costs you clicks and a useful relevance signal, so it is not free of consequence, but it is not a blocker to being listed or verified.
Do I need a website to put my business on Google?
No. You claim and verify a Business Profile and you are on Google, usually within days once verification completes. If you are not showing up, the cause is almost always verification, category, or address settings rather than the absence of a site. Google also retired the free websites it used to build from profiles, so that shortcut no longer exists.
Do I need a website for my cleaning business?
For residential work, a claimed profile with consistent reviews will carry you a long way and should come first. For commercial contracts, yes, effectively. Property and facilities managers want to see insurance, a service checklist, and evidence you handle buildings before they will award recurring work, and none of that fits on a profile. A quote form also removes the phone tag that loses residential bookings.
Do I need a website for my coaching or bookkeeping business?
For coaching, yes, and it is close to the whole business, because there is no local map result to win and the sale is trust in a person that has to be built in long form. For bookkeeping, yes for a different reason: clients are about to give you access to their money and they will check credentials, scope, and how you handle data before they call. In both cases one strong page with real proof beats a large site with none.
Is a Facebook page enough instead of a website?
It can be, early on, and plenty of businesses start there. The risks are that it sits behind a login prompt, the layout and reach change without your say, and the page can be disabled with no appeal. Use it for reach and posting, and get an address you own as soon as the business can justify it, even if that address is a single well-built page.
