How to Follow Up on a Quote Without Sounding Desperate

Most quotes do not get rejected. They get forgotten. Here is the timing, the three messages, and the wording that bring a stalled estimate back to life, without a single pushy line.

The quote you sent is not dead, it is just quiet

You sent the estimate. The customer said they would think about it. Then nothing. A week goes by and you tell yourself they went with someone else, so you move on to the next job and never look back.

Usually that is not what happened. They got busy. They meant to reply. Your quote slid under the school pickup, the work deadline, and the cousin who offered to do it cheaper. Nobody said no. Nobody said anything. The deal died of neglect, and the neglect was shared.

I run a one-person California field-services company, and over 700 jobs have gone through a system I built to follow up while I am out working. The biggest lesson from watching it run is boring: the follow-up you are afraid will annoy people mostly rescues work you had written off.

The principle: a quote is a question you have not finished asking

A quote is not a delivery. It is an open question: do you want to do this? Sending the number and going silent leaves the question hanging, and the person with the problem is not obligated to close it for you.

Following up is how you finish asking. It is also a service. You are helping a busy person make a decision they already half made. Think of it as keeping their problem on the table, not chasing their money.

Why speed and persistence both matter

There is real research on how quickly interest fades. A well-known Harvard Business Review study of 1.25 million sales leads found that firms that contacted a lead within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify it as firms that waited even an hour longer, and far more likely than those that waited a day or more. That study is about first contact, but the lesson carries over to quotes: attention decays fast, and the business that stays in front of the customer gets the decision.

I wrote about the first-response side in the 5-minute rule. This post is about what happens after you have sent the number, which is where most small businesses quietly lose the job.

Persistence matters because the decision does not happen on your schedule. It happens whenever the customer has a calm ten minutes. If your message is the one sitting at the top of their inbox at that moment, you win. If you only wrote once, you are betting on luck.

The three-message sequence

Here is the sequence I use and recommend for most service work. Adjust the days for your sales cycle. A roofing job is slower than a haircut.

Message one, day one or two: confirm receipt and make it easy. Hi Maria, just checking that the estimate for the fence came through okay. Happy to answer any questions or adjust the scope. That is it. No pressure, one clear offer to help.

Message two, day four or five: add something useful. Hi Maria, one thing worth mentioning on the fence: the gate hardware on the quote is the heavy-duty version, which tends to matter in the Valley heat. If you want a lighter option to bring the number down, I can send that too. You are giving a reason to reply that is not just checking in.

Message three, day ten to fourteen: close the loop politely. Hi Maria, I do not want to keep pinging you. If the timing is not right, no problem at all. I will hold your quote for a couple more weeks in case you want to move ahead. This works because it gives permission to say no, and it creates a soft deadline without inventing a fake one. People often reply to this one when they ignored the first two.

What to say, and what to leave out

Lead with the customer's situation, not your invoice. Mention the specific job, the date they wanted it done, the thing they said mattered. Specifics prove a human wrote it and remind them why they asked.

Offer a next step that is small. Reply with a yes and I will get you on the calendar is easier than let me know. Give one question they can answer in five words.

Leave out the guilt. Never write I have not heard back from you. Never fake scarcity unless it is real. A real deadline, like a material price that expires or a crew schedule that fills, is honest and useful. An invented one burns trust, and trust is most of what a local business has.

Pick the channel that matches how they contacted you

If they texted, text back. If they emailed, email. If they called, a short text after the call is usually welcome, and a voicemail is a fine third touch. Switching channels is a decent move on the final message, since a different channel can reach someone who buried the first. For many homeowners and small shops, a text gets read in minutes while an email waits for a week.

Keep each message short enough to read on a phone without scrolling. Two or three sentences, one question.

Trade by trade, a few real examples

Contractors and home services: the quote is often the first of several the customer is collecting, so message two should add value, like a note on materials, scheduling, or what you will do about the permit. The after-hours call that never became a quote is a related leak, covered in after-hours answering.

Photographers, event pros, and caterers: inquiries arrive months ahead. A day-three nudge and a day-ten nudge, plus a date-based note when the calendar is filling, protects the booking.

Law, tax, and professional offices: lean on clarity. Restate the scope and what happens next. People hesitate on professional services because they are unsure what they are buying, so the follow-up that answers one unasked question wins the file.

Notaries, mobile services, and one-visit jobs: the window is short, so follow up the same day, then once more. Slow follow-up here is just a no.

Put it on rails

The reason owners do not follow up is not laziness. It is the awkwardness, plus the fact that there is no moment in the day when it feels like the job. The fix is to take the decision out of your hands. When a quote goes out, the system starts a clock. If there is no reply, the next message fires on day two, day five, and day twelve, from your number, in your voice. When the customer replies, the sequence stops on its own, so you never double-message someone who already said yes.

Open your sent folder or your notes and pull every quote from the last sixty days that never got a clear answer. Send message three to all of them today. It is the cheapest sales work you will do this month, because those customers already know you and already asked.

FAQ

How long should I wait before following up on a quote?
One to two days for a first check that the quote arrived, then again around day four or five, then a final polite close at ten to fourteen days. Shorter cycles for quick jobs like single-visit services, longer for big projects.
How many times should I follow up on an estimate?
Three touches is a good default for most service work. After a polite final message that gives permission to say no, stop. Anything beyond that without a reply is rarely worth the damage to the relationship.
Should I follow up by text or email?
Use the channel the customer used with you. For many local customers a text is read faster than an email. Switching channels on the final message can reach someone who missed the first two.
What if the customer says my price is too high?
Ask what they were expecting and offer a lighter scope to hit a number, rather than cutting your rate on the same work. A smaller option protects your margin and gives them a real choice.
Can I automate quote follow-up without sounding robotic?
Yes. Write the messages yourself in your own voice, personalize with the customer's name and job, and let a system send them on a schedule. The sequence should stop the moment the customer replies.
